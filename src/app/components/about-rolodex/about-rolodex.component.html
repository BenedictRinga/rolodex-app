What is LoopKeeper?

LoopKeeper is a relationship follow-through assistant — the antidote to “I keep meaning to.” Choose one person who matters and set a rhythm — lunch every month, a call now and then — and when the moment comes, LoopKeeper nudges you, helps you close the loop, and lights the streak so the care becomes something you can see. One person at a time, never the whole address book: one small, finished win, then the next. The idea at its heart is older than any app — wind the wheel back to 1958.

The original Rolodex — 1958

One card per person — and now the card writes back.

Young'uns today may not know how crucial it was that a treasured secretary kept the records — connections, relationships, dates, appointments — for the best executives. Nothing forgotten, everything moving smoothly, always.

Before software, the remembering lived on a desk. The Rolodex — Arnold Neustadter's rolling index, patented 1956 and on desks everywhere by 1958 — sat beside the telephone of every capable secretary. One card per person: a name, a number, a penciled note — prefers mornings, ask about the daughter. Flip the wheel, find the face, make the call. The card carried the details so the person at the desk could carry the relationship.

What came next were plastic pocket folders with card slots — the Rolodex name survived across countless iterations. At first they lived on the secretary's desk, the quiet center of the executive suite. Then they became smaller and handier, and every businessperson kept one or several of their own — or synced theirs with an assistant's.

LoopKeeper is that idea, still working: one card per person, holding their story. What's new is that the card answers back — after every send, the Assistant writes what happened onto it, so the next nudge arrives already knowing. A Rolodex that writes back. You supply the caring; the card does the remembering.

Why it exists

Nearly everyone carries the same quiet debt: the friend you keep meaning to call back, the thank-you that died in drafts, the birthday that slipped while you meant to notice. Nobody stopped caring — life got loud, and somewhere between intending and doing, the fire went cold. An unfinished thing nags at the mind until it's done; psychology has known that since Zeigarnik, 1927. LoopKeeper exists to finish the small ones before they cost anything: a rhythm set once, a nudge when it's due, a message ready the minute you finally have two free minutes. The resolve it offers is simple — the people you mean to keep will hear from you while it still counts.

The human case — blight, questions, the map

The universal blight: every one of us carries the same quiet debt — a reply owed, a birthday missed, a congratulations that died in drafts, a friend we keep meaning to check on. It is not a productivity problem. It is a human one: life gets loud, the fire goes cold, and the relationship cools with it. Everyone in this room has felt it this week. That is the wedge — the most common failure of the human condition that software has never once solved, because software filed contacts instead of keeping relationships.

The Assistant's first questions — the psychology of a top-notch secretary:

  • When did you first meet this person?
  • What do you owe them — a reply, a thanks, a congratulations, an apology?
  • Why do they matter to you, really?
  • Where did you meet, and what was the energy of that room?
  • What did you promise, even silently?
  • What are they living through right now — the new job, the sick parent, the dream they told you about?
  • What would they feel if you reached out today? What would they feel if you waited another year?
  • Where does this relationship stand in five years if neither of you does anything?

That last question is the one people answer with a wince. LoopKeeper is built to make the wince unnecessary.

The vast human mapping: win the follow-up loop and you own the map of human relationships — who knows whom, who owes whom, who has gone cold, who matters most, who is one nudge from alive again. Every card is a node; every loop is an edge; every Assistant interaction is a signal. The network layer turns each user's kept relationships into the most valuable graph a consumer AI company could build — earned one tiny loop at a time.

The yardstick — the curve that decides everything

% of users still active on day N. Every consumer app decays — the green line bends, the red one falls off a cliff. The moment the green line runs parallel to the gold asymptote, the business is real: people come back because each day has a tiny loop waiting to be closed. Every feature we ship is judged against this chart — does it flatten it? That is the whole job.
