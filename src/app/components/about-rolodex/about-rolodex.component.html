What is Rolodex?

Rolodex is your confidential secretary — the contact manager that remembers, prompts, and communicates for you. It keeps every person you care about moving: birthdays, anniversaries, milestones, follow-ups, and the message you were going to send anyway — proffered, ready, one tap from Send.

The original Rolodex — 1958

The desk secretary's memory — 1958.

Long before the app, the Rolodex was a real desk tool — a rotary card file that sat beside the telephone of every capable secretary. Invented by Arnold Neustadter (patented 1956, on desks by 1958), its name blends rolling and index. One card per person: name, number, notes. Flip the wheel, find the person, keep the relationship moving.

RolodexAI is that same promise, digitized: the card still holds the person's story, but now the confidential secretary also remembers the reply you owe, the birthday you missed, and the follow-up that keeps a relationship alive. The wheel became an app — the secretary became the Confidante.

Why it exists

Contacts were never meant to sit still in a phonebook. Rolodex came out of Zyppar (2026) with one idea: the relationship is the product. The card is the surface — flip to a person and everything lives there: chat, reminders, follow-ups, their story.

Some doors are opened with a word. The roadmap lives behind it.

Thesis: "The biggest consumer AI opportunity is helping people close the tiny loops they keep avoiding. Emailing back the person you owe a reply… an agent that does the hard 90% of each loop, finds the context, makes the call, drafts the message, so all you do is hit send. The wedge is going after one loop first and nailing it."

Our wedge: the owed-reply loop — the person you keep meaning to write back. The most universal tiny loop there is. The confidante does the hard 90%: context, call, draft — you hit Send.

The universal blight: every one of us carries the same quiet debt — a reply owed, a birthday missed, a congratulations that died in drafts, a friend we keep meaning to check on. It is not a productivity problem. It is a human one: life gets loud, the fire goes cold, and the relationship cools with it. Everyone in this room has felt it this week. That is the wedge — the most common failure of the human condition that software has never once solved, because software filed contacts instead of keeping relationships.

The Confidante's first questions — the psychology of a top-notch secretary:

  • When did you last contact this person?
  • What do you owe them — a reply, a thanks, a congratulations, an apology?
  • Why do they matter to you, really?
  • Where did you meet, and what was the energy of that room?
  • What did you promise, even silently?
  • What are they living through right now — the new job, the sick parent, the dream they told you about?
  • What would they feel if you reached out today? What would they feel if you waited another year?
  • Where does this relationship stand in five years if neither of you does anything?

That last question is the one people answer with a wince. RolodexAI is built to make the wince unnecessary.

The vast human mapping: win the follow-up loop and you own the map of human relationships — who knows whom, who owes whom, who has gone cold, who matters most, who is one nudge from alive again. Every card is a node; every loop is an edge; every Confidante interaction is a signal. The network layer turns each user's kept relationships into the most valuable graph a consumer AI company could build — earned one tiny loop at a time.

The yardstick — the curve that decides everything

% of users still active on day N. Every consumer app decays — the green line bends, the red one falls off a cliff. The moment the green line runs parallel to the gold asymptote, the business is real: people come back because each day has a tiny loop waiting to be closed. Every feature we ship is judged against this chart — does it flatten it? That is the whole job.
