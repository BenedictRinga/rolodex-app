The human case — blight, questions, the map

The universal blight: every one of us carries the same quiet debt — a reply owed, a birthday missed, a congratulations that died in drafts, a friend we keep meaning to check on. It is not a productivity problem. It is a human one: life gets loud, the fire goes cold, and the relationship cools with it. Everyone in this room has felt it this week. That is the wedge — the most common failure of the human condition that software has never once solved, because software filed contacts instead of keeping relationships.

The Assistant's first questions — the psychology of a top-notch secretary:

  • When did your story with this person begin?
  • What do you owe them — a reply, a thanks, a congratulations, an apology?
  • Why do they matter to you, really?
  • Where did you meet, and what was the energy of that room?
  • What did you promise, even silently?
  • What are they living through right now — the new job, the sick parent, the dream they told you about?
  • What would they feel if you reached out today? What would they feel if you waited another year?
  • Where does this relationship stand in five years if neither of you does anything?

That last question is the one people answer with a wince. LoopKeeper is built to make the wince unnecessary.

The vast human mapping: win the follow-up loop and you own the map of human relationships — who knows whom, who owes whom, who has gone cold, who matters most, who is one nudge from alive again. Every card is a node; every loop is an edge; every Assistant interaction is a signal. The network layer turns each user's kept relationships into the most valuable graph a consumer AI company could build — earned one tiny loop at a time.

The yardstick — the curve that decides everything

% of users still active on day N. Every consumer app decays — the green line bends, the red one falls off a cliff. The moment the green line runs parallel to the gold asymptote, the business is real: people come back because each day has a tiny loop waiting to be closed. Every feature we ship is judged against this chart — does it flatten it? That is the whole job.

RedesignThe contactless framework

LoopKeeper is not a safer rolodex.
It is a tray for unfinished acts whose counterparties
the software is not allowed to know.

The mistake the rolodex baked in

A rolodex starts from people and hopes tasks will appear. LoopKeeper has to start from tension and treat people as optional decoration the user keeps at home.

What actually occupies the mind is not "Priya, +254…, last emailed 12 March." It is:

  • "I owe a reply."
  • "I promised a file."
  • "I said we would talk after the trip."
  • "I have not decided."

None of those sentences require a server or the Play Store permission READ_CONTACTS. The identity lives in the user's head. The product's job is to hold the incompleteness, not the person.

The science we stand on

Zeigarnik: interrupted work stays warmer in memory than finished work. Ovsiankina: people feel a quasi-need to resume it. Masicampo & Baumeister: a specific plan discharges much of the tension before the task is even done.

A private LoopKeeper therefore has exactly three legal moves:

  1. Hold the open loop, so the user does not have to.
  2. Resume it with the next physical action — draft, send, call, show up.
  3. Release it with a plan, a drop, or a close — so the mind can let go.

The waiter in the original studies remembered that table 4 still wants the bill — not a national ID.

The threat model, said bluntly

Assume every assurance we write will be disbelieved.

If this exists on a server we controlA careful user treats it as
Address book dumpA copy of their social world
Phone numbers / emailsA deanonymizing key
"We'll encrypt it"A promise, not a property
"We don't sell data"Revocable
AI that "knows your network"The thing they already fear

The only pitch that survives: we cannot name your people, because we never receive their names.

That is not a copy change. It is a data-flow change — and if testers must believe the privacy policy before the app is useful, we have already lost. The architecture must make a lie structurally hard: LoopKeeper should do its job on a device that has never seen a contact record.

The object we should have been storing

Replace Contact with Loop. A Loop is:

  • a verb — "reply", "send", "decide", "show up", "drop"
  • a handle the user invented — "the architect", "Ma's doctor", "Thursday slot"
  • a shard of context they typed or pasted
  • a state — open / parked / waiting / closed / dropped
  • a next move that does not require resolving a person in any database

The handle is not an identity. It is a mnemonic, like a tab name. Two users can both have a loop called "the architect" — the system should not care, and should not be able to join them.

The standing rules

  • Contacts permission: never request it.
  • Account: optional.
  • Cloud: optional — and if it exists, it stores ciphertext the server cannot read.

The three storage tiers, shipped in order

Tier A — device-only. Loops live on the phone. No account. Uninstall = gone, unless they exported. This is the version you hand your children.

Tier B — user-encrypted sync. A passphrase they type, never stored. The client encrypts the blob; the server sees an opaque ID and a timestamp. The passphrase cannot be reset — that pain is the proof.

Tier C — sealed send packet. If they ever insist on in-app send, the address is entered in that session, used, and not written unless they opt into a local alias. Default: the address is never persisted.

How the product works with zero contacts

1. The user names the ghost. Capture is one line, on purpose: "Reply to the gallery woman about Thursday." On device that becomes — verb: reply · handle: gallery woman · object: Thursday · state: open. Autocomplete runs against their own previous handles, never the address book. The moment an OS contact picker appears, we have asked for the graph.

2. Identity stays in the apps they already trust. Mail, WhatsApp, Messages, Phone already have the names. LoopKeeper is a staging room, not a new directory: open a loop → the draft is written → tap Copy or Share → the native app sends → come back and mark sent / waiting / closed. We lose "one-tap send." We gain the only thing that gets family onto a closed test: this thing cannot text my children for me, because it does not know them.

3. Context without a dossier. Context is whatever the user parks next to the loop — a dictated sentence, a screenshot kept on device, a voice memo. If a model runs in the cloud it sees: Loop type: overdue reply. Tone: short, apologetic. Topic: Thursday slot. Handle: [redacted]. — never a name and a number. Redaction is a user-facing control: a "preview what the model will see" screen is worth more than a privacy policy.

4. The AI that does not need a graph. Find context = read this loop's own notes. Make the call = recommend send / park / drop from loop age and verb, not from who they are. Draft = fill a template from the loop text the user already wrote. Remember the person = do not. Remember the unfinished act. "Know your network" is a marketing phrase we kill on purpose.

Zeigarnik machinery that never asks who

  • The board is capped at three live loops. Forty-seven open names is why people avoid the app and the tasks. Three is a working memory. The rest wait in a sealed backlog.
  • Start the act for them. Every card already contains a first sentence of the draft — a blank card is a new task; a half-written card is an interrupted one, and interrupted is what the mind returns to.
  • Plan-based release. "Saturday after the match, copy the draft into WhatsApp." Tension drops with a plan — no identity required. This is how we avoid becoming a guilt machine.
  • Close must feel like putting a plate down. The waiters forgot the order once it was paid. The close animation and the card's disappearance are the discharge — closed loops never linger as a social profile.
  • Handles may stay opaque forever. "The man from the gallery" is a complete key for its owner. Completeness meters are Zeigarnik used against the user. Banned.

Features that get stranger — and still work

Role loops"The school." "The landlord." "The clinic." Institutions are what people avoid — and they were never in the address book as one record anyway.
Self-loops"Decide whether I am going." Much avoided work has no counterparty at all. Contacts-first design cannot even see this category. It may be the real wedge.
Object loops"The permission slip." "The rebate." The person is incidental.
Place-shaped loopsGeofence the user's own chosen place: "when I get to the office, show the three parked sends." Their movement as a cue — nobody's contacts involved.
Time-shaped loops"Sunday 8pm is loop hour." A ritual beats a graph. Family testers understand a ritual.
Shared loop, no shared identityPairing is a one-time code, like a padlock — not an address-book merge. If either side refuses, the loop stays single-player.
Photograph of the debtSnap the paper note, the text thread. On-device OCR, then the image stays local or dies. The loop is born from evidence they already had.
Dead-drop draftsTheir existing mail client is the identity layer. LoopKeeper is a typewriter for unfinished speech.
An anonymous streak"3 loops put down this week" — a number a tester can show anyone without exposing who they know.
Deliberate forgettingAn old planless loop is offered for burial. Helping people drop is as much the product as helping them send.

What gets ripped out for the tester build

  • Import from Google / Apple contacts
  • "Find people in your network"
  • Birthday / job-change scraping
  • Relationship maps
  • "Who should I reach out to?" ranked by graph
  • Cloud backup of names by default
  • Login-with-Google that then offers the address book
  • Any onboarding step that previews a permission dialog

Every one of those is a reason our own children will not tap Install.

The tester pitch, rewritten

What we sent was probably: try my app, it helps you stay in touch. What they heard was: give this thing the family. Say this instead:

LoopKeeper never reads your contacts. It cannot. There is no contact permission.
You type a nickname only you understand.
The AI sees a redacted task, not a person.
You send from the app you already use.
Uninstall and it is gone.

Then give them a 30-second first loop that is not social: "Park the thing I keep not deciding." Once the app has held one private tension and released it, staying opted in for 14 days is a small favor instead of a character test.

Play Console facts: twelve opted-in-and-still-opted-in testers for fourteen continuous days. Invites are not testers; dropouts reset us. Over-recruit, and make staying cheap. A no-contacts build is how staying becomes cheap.

The build order that matches the constraint

Week 0
The closed-test binary. No READ_CONTACTS. No account. Capture → three-slot board → draft → share sheet → mark closed/parked/dropped. On-device only. One screen: "network calls this session: 0."
Week 2
If they still have it installed. Plan-based park ("Saturday after the match"). Redaction preview if any model is used. Export / wipe.
Later
Only if testers ask. Passphrase sync. Optional last-mile send that never persists addresses. Pairing codes for two-sided loops. — Never promised in the invite; promises about "send later" reawaken the fear.
